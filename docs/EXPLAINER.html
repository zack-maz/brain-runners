Three minds, one tunnel

A fruit fly's brain, a small AI that answers yes/no questions, and a chatbot all play the same video game. We watch how each one thinks while it plays.

Level 1 · no background needed

1. The idea in one minute

There are very different kinds of "mind" around now. Big chatbots write text. Smaller specialised models give judgments as numbers. And scientists have mapped every neuron and connection in a fruit fly's brain, which you can now run as a simulation on a laptop.

We wanted to see them side by side, doing the same simple task, with their thinking visible. So we built:

  • a tiny game in which a runner goes down a tunnel and must not fall into holes;
  • three players: a fruit fly brain, Jev, and an LLM (Claude Haiku 4.5);
  • a player page where you watch all three run the same tunnel together, with a panel under each one showing what it "had in mind" at every step.

The fun part is that the three are completely different inside. The fly was never trained on anything: it plays with the escape reflexes wiring gave it. Jev answers precise questions with probabilities. The LLM reads a description of what's ahead and replies with a move.

2. The game

It is modelled on the browser game Run. Imagine a tunnel whose wall is made of floor tiles. The runner moves forward one row at a time. Some tiles are missing: those are gaps. Land on a gap and you fall. Your score is how many rows you survive, up to 150 (up to 300 in the first version of the game).

  • The tunnel has 12 lanes in a ring: step past the last lane and you are on the first again (in the real game, the tunnel rotates).
  • Each turn a player picks one of four moves: left, right, stay, jump.
  • Everyone can see the next 6 rows, three lanes either side. More would not help: on v1 a perfect player finishes 98% of tracks with 6 rows and no more with 8 or 10; on v2 it finishes 97% with 6, 98.5% with 8, 99% with 10 (seeds 1000–1199).
  • Tracks come from a seed (a number). Same seed, same track, for every player. The track gets harder as you go (more and wider gaps), but there is always at least one way through, so every fall is the player's fault.
  • The game comes in versions. v1 is the one phases 1 to 5 were played on: 300 rows, the gaps thicken slowly over all 300. v2, the default since 2026-09-21, is 150 rows and reaches full difficulty by row 100, so the players pull apart sooner and a paid run costs half as much. A seed is a different track in each version, so runs of different versions never share a scoreboard. How far players see is part of the version too; an experiment can change it (--lookahead, --window) and the version's name then says so (v2+look3).
  • The game is turn-based: it waits for each player to decide. Nobody loses for being slow, which matters because the fly simulation takes about a second per move.

3. The three minds

FLY

A fruit fly brain

A simulation of the whole adult fruit fly brain: about 139,000 neurons and 15 million connections, mapped by scientists (the FlyWire project) and published as a runnable model.

Untrained. We only plug in: holes ahead become "something is looming" signals into its eyes, and we read the neurons that make a real fly turn or jump away.

JEV

Jev (TypeSafe)

A "System One" model: instead of writing text, it answers questions with probabilities.

We ask it four yes/no questions per step: "would left land on a gap?", the same for stay, right and jump. A few lines of our code then pick the move it thinks is least likely to fall.

LLM

Claude Haiku 4.5

A regular chatbot-style model. It gets the rules and a description of the rows ahead, and answers with one move.

The one-shot LLM (llm) is told exactly the same rules, in the same words, as the one-shot Jev. Its question-set twins (llm_composed and so on) ask the same questions as their Jev, under the same rule, but three things still differ between the two models — see below.

Beside the composed Jev shown above, there are seven more question-set players: two more ways to ask Jev (jev_choice, jev_two_step, jev_reader) and a Claude Haiku twin for every set, including the composed one (llm_composed, llm_choice, llm_two_step, llm_reader), covered in section 10.

There are also three yardsticks that are not contestants: random (picks any move), always_jump, and solver, a perfect little search program that plays with the same 6-row view and almost always reaches the end. They tell us what "bad" and "as good as possible" look like.

What we learned along the way:

  • The fly really does dodge. Without any training it plays far above random, because its wiring steers away from looming things. It doesn't plan, so it sometimes dodges into another hole.
  • How you ask Jev matters enormously. Asked one broad question ("which move?"), Jev died after 23 rows. Asked four narrow yes/no questions, it read the track almost perfectly (1 wrong answer in 928) and survived 247. That's why the demo uses the "composed" version.
  • The LLM's typical death is stepping sideways into a hole when running straight was safe.
  • Composed Jev "wanders": when every move is safe its four answers are nearly equal, so it often steps sideways for no reason. It never killed it, but on track 1001 it probably cost it the finish.
  • Cost is tiny. An LLM move costs about 0.0006 USD; a Jev move about 0.00003 USD (an estimate). The fly runs on the laptop for free.

5. How to watch it

Everything runs from the terminal in the project folder. Watching costs nothing.

A recorded run

uv run python -m bakeoff view runs/20260919-151934 runs/20260920-102919 runs/20260921-120903

This writes one HTML file (the demo player) and you open it in a browser. It shows the tunnel with three pixel runners (a fly, an orange critter for the LLM, and "the visor" for Jev), a strip of "mind panels" under it, and a play bar. Keys: space plays, arrows step a row, 1/2/3 pick whose mind is in focus.

Live

uv run python -m bakeoff live --game v1 --seed 1001

This plays a track in real time on your Mac and streams it into the same page. With no --max-requests it spends nothing: Jev's and the LLM's answers come from the saved cache of the earlier run (which was played on game v1, hence --game v1; on a track nobody has played, a paid player with no budget stops at its first question), and the fly is simulated again for real (about a minute to build its brain, then about a second per row).

Level 2 · some programming background

8. How it's built

A Python package, bakeoff/, plus a plain-JavaScript page in viewer/. The data flows one way:

The pieces

  • Track and engine (bakeoff/game/) are pure code: no files, no network, and randomness only from the seed. The generator first carves one legal random walk through the track and keeps those tiles as floor, then scatters gaps whose rate rises from 4% to 16% per lane and whose width grows to 3 (over 300 rows in v1, over 100 in v2). Rows 0–4 are an empty runway. Every number of a game version lives in one record, Rules (bakeoff/game/rules.py), which each run writes into its meta.json.
  • Senses (bakeoff/senses.py) turn the game state into two encodings of the same view: JSON for Jev and the LLM, and two firing rates (left eye, right eye) for the fly.
  • Players all share one interface: reset(game, seed), act(senses) → Decision, observe(action). A Decision carries the move plus everything we want to see later: the questions, answers, latency, tokens, cache hit, and the fly's neuron readings.
  • The runner plays players × seeds and streams one JSON line per move into a run directory. live uses the very same play_row function, one row at a time for all players in lockstep.
  • Report, replay, view only read run directories. That's why watching is free.

The commands

uv run pytest                          # fast tests (Python + the viewer's JS via node --test)
uv run pytest -m slow                  # builds the real fly brain: ~1 GB, ~1 minute
uv run python -m scripts.fetch_fly_data   # once: fly model + annotations into data/
uv run python -m bakeoff run    --players fly,random,solver --seeds 20 --seed-start 1000   # [--game v1] [--lookahead N]
uv run python -m bakeoff report runs/<id>
uv run python -m bakeoff view   runs/<id> [runs/<id> ...]
uv run python -m bakeoff live   --seed 1001 --players fly,jev_composed,llm [--max-requests N]
Level 3 · the details

9. The fly, in detail

The model is Shiu et al., A Drosophila computational brain model reveals sensorimotor processing, Nature 2024 (code, MIT, Brian2), using FlyWire v783 connectivity: 138,639 leaky integrate-and-fire neurons and 15.1 million synapses, default parameters. It has no learning, no spontaneous activity and no visual front end: you choose which neurons to drive and read which neurons fire.

One decision

  1. Eyes in. Every visible gap adds 250 / row³ Hz to an eye (row 1 = next row, so near gaps dominate). Gaps left of the runner feed the left eye, right of it the right eye, straight ahead both. Each eye is capped at 250 Hz and rounded to 25 Hz steps.
  2. Stimulate. Restore the network to its clean state, then drive the looming-detector neurons of each eye (LPLC2 and LC4: 162 cells on the left, 152 on the right) with Poisson spikes at those rates.
  3. Simulate 100 ms of brain time (about a second of wall clock on the M1).
  4. Read out.
    • Turn signal = (DNa01 + DNb01) right − (DNa01 + DNb01) left. These steering neurons fire on the side opposite a one-sided threat. Above 0 Hz → right, below → left.
    • Jump signal = mean rate of the Giant Fiber (DNp01), the fly's real escape-jump neuron. Above 200 Hz → jump, which beats a turn.
    • Otherwise stay.
Ours, not the fly's

Four numbers: the gain (250 Hz) and falloff (cubic) of the eye input, and the two thresholds (0 Hz and 200 Hz). They were picked once from 768 candidates by a rule fixed in advance, on practice seeds 1000–1199, using a measured "response surface" as a fast stand-in for the brain, then checked against the real brain (stand-in predicted 118.7 average rows on seeds 1000–1019, the real brain did 118.0). Frozen: calibration/REPORT.md. They must never be retuned.

Known weaknesses (said on screen too)
  • It doesn't plan; it flees and may flee into another gap.
  • Input is crude: whole-eye stimulation, not a picture.
  • It leans right: on equal input to both eyes the turn signal is 0 to +20 Hz, never negative (its left eye has more looming cells). With a 0 Hz threshold, a hole straight ahead usually becomes a step right. The lean is the fly's; the threshold that exposes it is ours.
  • Its Giant Fiber fires at 100–200 Hz in the model, unrealistic for a real fly (about once per escape); we use it as a graded signal.
  • The steering role of DNa01/DNb01 comes from published studies, not from anything we verified.

Randomness only comes from the Poisson input, seeded per (track, row), so a fly run repeats exactly: the live run on track 1001 was move for move the same as the batch run two days earlier.

10. Jev and the LLM, in detail

The one-shot pair, Jev and the LLM, are both told the same rules in the same words (bakeoff/players/briefing.py, written before any paid request) and receive the same JSON view:

{
  "lane": 4, "lanes": 12, "rows_survived": 37,
  "ahead": [
    {"row": 1, "gaps_relative": [-1, 0]},
    {"row": 2, "gaps_relative": []},
    {"row": 3, "gaps_relative": [2, 3]}, ...
  ],
  "actions": {"left": "move one lane left", "right": "move one lane right",
              "jump": "clear the next row, land on the one after", "stay": "run straight"}
}

The LLM (llm)

Claude Haiku 4.5, one request per row, system prompt = the rules, user message = the JSON, answer forced to {"action": "left" | "right" | "jump" | "stay"} by structured output. Anything else is logged as invalid.

Composed Jev (jev_composed, the one in the demo)

One request per row with four Nouls (a Noul is Jev's yes/no-as-probability question type):

gap_left : Would the action `left` land the runner on a gap, that is,
           does `ahead[0].gaps_relative` contain -1?
gap_stay : ... `stay` ... ahead[0] contains 0?
gap_right: ... `right` ... ahead[0] contains 1?
gap_jump : ... `jump` ... ahead[1] contains 0?

Code picks the action with the lowest probability after rounding to two decimals; ties go in the solver's order stay, left, right, jump. If any answer is missing, the move is invalid. The visor sprite's lit slit shows 1 minus the chosen action's probability, that is, how sure Jev is the move is safe.

Ours, not TypeSafe's

The question wording and the pick-the-lowest rule. It looks one step ahead only. The rounding is why it wanders: on safe rows answers like 0.03 and 0.04 rarely tie, so the "safest" move is often a sideways step.

One-shot Jev (jev, kept for comparison)

One Choice over the four actions plus two logging Nouls. Spike 02 showed why it failed: on the 55 dangerous states of track 1000 its Choice landed on a gap 29% of the time, about the same as always staying (33%). Jev's questions in one request run in parallel and can't see each other's answers, so the Nouls couldn't help the Choice. TypeSafe's own docs call the broad question the anti-pattern.

Update 2a: the question-set twins

jev_choice, jev_two_step and jev_reader ask Jev three other ways; each has a Claude Haiku twin (llm_choice, llm_two_step, llm_reader) asked the same questions under the same rule. The model is not the only difference between a twin and its Jev: the LLM twin is also given the briefing of the rules (Jev's yes/no questions carry only the question, except in the choice set), Jev's answers in one request are made in parallel and cannot see each other while the LLM writes them in one reply, and the LLM's probabilities are numbers it states rather than Jev's own probability. Paid runs are done for practice seeds 1000–1004 (docs/COSTS.md, "Update 2a").

Money safety

Every paid request goes through bakeoff/clients/core.py: first a disk cache (key = sha256 of provider, model, senses and the full question set, so a changed prompt can never reuse an old answer), then a hard cap (--max-requests, per paid player, default 0 = replay only), then the live call, with SDK retries off so the cap is exact. A provider failure is logged as an error and the game runs stay (never the solver's move; being rescued would hide exactly what we want to see). Keys load from a git-ignored .env. The CLI refuses a live paid run on seeds below 1000 without --tournament.

11. Runs, records and replays

A run directory (runs/<timestamp>/) holds meta.json (status, git commit, arguments, model ids, fly constants) and one <player>.jsonl file with one line per move. Status goes running → completed | aborted | budget_exhausted | interrupted.

Each record mixes two moments: what the player saw when deciding (row, lane, senses, looming, questions) and what happened after (answers, chosen_action, executed_action, solver_action, alive, rows_survived, death_cause, latency_ms, usage, cache_hit, info). The full track is logged once per seed. Contract: docs/STEP_RECORD.md.

bakeoff/replay.py merges several run directories into one replay object (docs/REPLAY_DATA.md): tracks, episodes lined up by row, landing tiles, the scoreboard and whether its means are a fair comparison. bakeoff/view.py embeds that object, the JavaScript and the two fonts in one HTML file that never loads anything from the network.

The viewer's JavaScript is split into pure, tested modules: timeline.js (time is rows; a jump takes two ticks), tunnel.js (tile geometry, fixed camera), sprites.js (pixel figures), stage.js (overlapping runners, auto-focus), minds.js (the panels) and feed.js, the only way frames reach app.js, either from the embedded replay or from the live stream. live serves the same page from 127.0.0.1 and pushes the same frame shapes over Server-Sent Events, standard library only.

12. Rules we hold ourselves to

  • Seed discipline. Seeds 1000+ are practice (1000–1399 were used to calibrate the fly). Tournament seeds are below 1000, and nothing is ever tuned on them. Prompts and rules are frozen before the tournament; bad results get named in the write-up, not fixed.
  • Honesty about the fly. Untrained, innate wiring only. Anything that is our mapping rather than its biology is labelled as ours, on screen and in the write-up.
  • Same words for Jev and the LLM, and the asymmetry (Jev gets four narrow questions, the LLM one) is named.
  • Budget. No paid request without a cap and a cache; no cap raised without your go-ahead.
  • One fly at a time on the 8 GB Mac (about 1 GB each).
  • How work gets done: brainstorm → spec → plan → one implementer and one reviewer per task → final review → PR. Never on main. Tests first; no test touches the network.
