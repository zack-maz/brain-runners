Three minds, one tunnel

Two fruit fly brains, a small AI that answers yes/no questions, and two chatbots all play the same video game. We watch how each one thinks while it plays.

Level 1 · no background needed

1. The idea in one minute

There are very different kinds of "mind" around now. Big chatbots write text. Smaller specialised models give judgments as numbers. And scientists have mapped every neuron and connection in a fruit fly's brain, which you can now run as a simulation on a laptop.

We wanted to see them side by side, doing the same simple task, with their thinking visible. So we built:

  • a tiny game in which a runner goes down a tunnel and must not fall into holes;
  • three kinds of player: two fruit fly brains (fly and fly2, connected to the game two different ways), Jev, and chatbots (Claude Haiku 4.5, and GLM Flash on a free tier);
  • a page that runs the show: you pick the track and the players in the browser, watch them run the same tunnel together with a panel under each one showing what it "had in mind" at every step, open each one's running log line by line, and read the scoreboard and the benchmark on a second tab.

The fun part is that the three kinds are completely different inside. Neither fly was ever trained on anything: each plays with the escape reflexes its wiring gave it, and the two share that wiring: they are connected to the game in different ways. Jev answers precise questions with probabilities. The LLM reads a description of what's ahead and replies with a move.

2. The game

It is modelled on the browser game Run. Imagine a tunnel whose wall is made of floor tiles. The runner moves forward one row at a time. Some tiles are missing: those are gaps. Land on a gap and you fall. Your score is how many rows you survive, up to 150 (up to 300 in the first version of the game).

  • The tunnel has 12 lanes in a ring: step past the last lane and you are on the first again (in the real game, the tunnel rotates).
  • Each turn a player picks one of four moves: left, right, stay, jump.
  • Everyone can see the next 6 rows, three lanes either side. More would not help: on v1 a perfect player finishes 98% of tracks with 6 rows and no more with 8 or 10; on v2 it finishes 97% with 6, 98.5% with 8, 99% with 10 (seeds 1000–1199).
  • Tracks come from a seed (a number). Same seed, same track, for every player. The track gets harder as you go (more and wider gaps), but there is always at least one way through, so every fall is the player's fault.
  • The game comes in versions. v1 is the one phases 1 to 5 were played on: 300 rows, the gaps thicken slowly over all 300. v2, the default since 2026-09-21, is 150 rows and reaches full difficulty by row 100, so the players pull apart sooner and a paid run costs half as much. A seed is a different track in each version, so runs of different versions never share a scoreboard. How far players see is part of the version too; an experiment can change it (--lookahead, --window) and the version's name then says so (v2+look3).
  • The game is turn-based: it waits for each player to decide. Nobody loses for being slow, which matters because the fly simulation takes about a second per move.

3. The minds

FLY

A fruit fly brain

A simulation of the whole adult fruit fly brain: about 139,000 neurons and 15 million connections, mapped by scientists (the FlyWire project) and published as a runnable model.

Untrained. We only plug in: holes ahead become "something is looming" signals into its eyes, and we read the neurons that make a real fly turn or jump away.

FLY2

A second fruit fly brain

The same brain, connected to the game a different way: gaps beside the runner's own lane, not just ahead of it, also drive eye cells (a "sideways channel"), and its turn is read from a different set of steering neurons.

Untrained, like fly. This connection was one of three candidates measured on the real brain and picked by a rule fixed before any of them were measured.

JEV

Jev (TypeSafe)

A "System One" model: instead of writing text, it answers questions with probabilities.

We ask it four yes/no questions per step: "would left land on a gap?", the same for stay, right and jump. A few lines of our code then pick the move it thinks is least likely to fall.

LLM

Claude Haiku 4.5

A regular chatbot-style model. It gets the rules and a description of the rows ahead, and answers with one move.

The plain LLM (haiku_plain, the one broad question) is told exactly the same rules, in the same words, as jev_plain. Its question-set twins (haiku_step1 and so on) ask the same questions as their Jev, under the same rule, but three things still differ between the two models — see below. A third model, Zhipu's GLM Flash, plays the same four sets on a free tier (glm_step1 and so on).

What became clear in phase 5 is that how you ask matters as much as who you ask. So the project is now a grid: four question sets (step1, guided, step2, map; plus plain, the one broad question) × three models (Jev, Claude Haiku, GLM Flash), each set worded once and played by every model under the same rule — jev_step1 and haiku_step1 and glm_step1 are the same questions asked of three different minds. Section 10 has the detail. Every question and every rule is ours, not the models'.

There are also three yardsticks that are not contestants: random (picks any move), always_jump, and solver, a perfect little search program that plays with the same 6-row view and almost always reaches the end. They tell us what "bad" and "as good as possible" look like.

It also counts its own luck: 36 pairs at 95% each means about 2 verdicts would appear by chance alone. That is the honest state of it.

What we learned along the way:

  • The fly really does dodge. Without any training it plays far above random, because its wiring steers away from looming things. It doesn't plan, so it sometimes dodges into another hole — on v2 all five of its deaths were jumps into a gap. (Its settings were frozen on the old game, v1, and never retuned, which costs it something here.)
  • How you ask matters as much as who you ask. Asked one broad question ("which move?"), Jev died after 23 rows. Asked four narrow yes/no questions, it read the track almost perfectly and finished. Asked to look two moves ahead, it became the best player in the bakeoff.
  • Sharp answers are what make the extra step pay. haiku_step2 gets exactly the same questions and the same rule as jev_step2, and scores worse than its own one-step twin: its probabilities are nearly three times less accurate (Brier 0.020 against 0.007).
  • A wording can fail on one tile. jev_map dies on every track for one reason: it reads the tile straight ahead as floor when it is a gap (44% of them), while the step1 wording for the same tile never missed once. That is our wording failing, not Jev failing to read the track.
  • Cost is small but not equal. Per row: the Jev players about 0.2 s (price not published), the Haiku twins 0.7–1.1 s and 0.0008–0.0016 USD, haiku_map 2.9 s and 0.0064 USD — eight times the other twins. The fly is free and takes about 0.7 s a row on the laptop.

5. How to watch it, and run it

Everything runs from the terminal in the project folder. Watching costs nothing. This section is the tour; docs/WALKTHROUGH.md is the manual, command by command and panel by panel.

A recorded run

uv run python -m bakeoff view runs/20260921-165433

This writes one HTML file and you open it in a browser. Nothing is fetched from the network: the page, the data, the fonts and the benchmark are all inside that one file, so it can be mailed to someone.

Live, with the page running the show

uv run python -m bakeoff live --port 8765

This opens a lobby on your own machine and prints its address. In the browser you pick the track and tick who plays, and the page tells you what the run would cost at worst before you press Start; the command keeps the ceiling, so nothing the page sends can spend more than the cap you started it with (the default cap is 0, which replays cached answers and spends nothing; Jev has no cap since decision 50, because its requests cost you nothing, and they are still counted and priced). Press Start and the tunnel fills in real time; when it ends, the page is ready for another track without restarting the command.

What is on the page

  • Run tab. The tunnel with its pixel runners; under it a mind panel per player showing what it was asked and what it answered on the row you are watching, and inside each panel an "Its log" list: one line per row, with the question count, the answer, how long it took (or cached), the move, and any error in red. The log follows the row on screen, so it never gives a replay's ending away. Below the tunnel, Players: who runs next, and who is in the tunnel.
  • Analysis tab. The level table (rows survived per track; pick a track to watch it), the scoreboard, the notes about what is ours rather than the fly's or the model's, and the benchmark — intervals, survival curves, rows against cost and time, and every pair of players. After a live run it fills in by itself with the numbers for the run that just finished.
  • Keys on the Run tab: space plays, arrows step a row, 1/2/3 pick whose mind is in focus.

The benchmark on its own

uv run python -m bakeoff bench runs/<id> [runs/<id>:player,player ...]

Scores runs that were already recorded (so it spends nothing), prints the tables in the terminal and writes bench.json and a standalone bench.html. It refuses to mix runs of different lengths, gives no interval below five tracks, and says how many of its verdicts chance alone would produce.

8. How it's built

A Python package, bakeoff/, plus a plain-JavaScript page in viewer/. The data flows one way:

The pieces

  • Track and engine (bakeoff/game/) are pure code: no files, no network, and randomness only from the seed. The generator first carves one legal random walk through the track and keeps those tiles as floor, then scatters gaps whose rate rises from 4% to 16% per lane and whose width grows to 3 (over 300 rows in v1, over 100 in v2). Rows 0–4 are an empty runway. Every number of a game version lives in one record, Rules (bakeoff/game/rules.py), which each run writes into its meta.json.
  • Senses (bakeoff/senses.py) turn the game state into two encodings of the same view: JSON for Jev and the LLM, and two firing rates (left eye, right eye) for the fly.
  • Players all share one interface: reset(game, seed), act(senses) → Decision, observe(action). A Decision carries the move plus everything we want to see later: the questions, answers, latency, tokens, cache hit, and the fly's neuron readings.
  • The runner plays players × seeds and streams one JSON line per move into a run directory. live uses the very same play_row function, one row at a time for all players in lockstep.
  • Report, replay, view, bench only read run directories. That's why watching is free, and why the benchmark can never change what a run recorded.
  • The live session (bakeoff/session.py) is what the page talks to: one per command, holding one budget per paid player for the whole session, the refusal rules (a paid player may not play a tournament seed without --tournament, cap or no cap) and one run at a time. The page's requests carry a token minted at startup and embedded in the page, which keeps other pages in the browser out; it is not a defence against another program on this machine, since whatever can fetch the page can read the token.
  • The benchmark (bakeoff/bench.py) scores recorded runs: 95% t intervals over tracks, paired differences for every pair of players, survival curves, and time and cost per row. The page and the standalone bench.html draw it with the same JavaScript renderer.

The commands

uv run pytest                          # fast tests (Python + the viewer's JS via node --test)
uv run pytest -m slow                  # builds the real fly brain: ~1 GB, ~1 minute
uv run python -m scripts.fetch_fly_data   # once: fly model + annotations into data/
uv run python -m bakeoff run    --players fly,fly2,random,solver --seeds 20 --seed-start 1000   # [--game v1] [--lookahead N]
uv run python -m bakeoff report runs/<id>
uv run python -m bakeoff view   runs/<id> [runs/<id> ...]          # one offline HTML page
uv run python -m bakeoff bench  runs/<id>[:player,...] [...]        # intervals, pairs, cost and time
uv run python -m bakeoff live   --port 8765                         # the lobby: the browser picks and starts
uv run python -m bakeoff live   --start --seed 1001 --players fly,fly2,jev_step1,haiku_plain [--max-requests N]

9. The fly, in detail

The model is Shiu et al., A Drosophila computational brain model reveals sensorimotor processing, Nature 2024 (code, MIT, Brian2), using FlyWire v783 connectivity: 138,639 leaky integrate-and-fire neurons and 15.1 million synapses, default parameters. It has no learning, no spontaneous activity and no visual front end: you choose which neurons to drive and read which neurons fire.

Two players read this same brain, connected to the game two different ways: fly, next, and fly2, a second way in and out, further down.

One decision

  1. Eyes in. Every visible gap adds 250 / row³ Hz to an eye (row 1 = next row, so near gaps dominate). Gaps left of the runner feed the left eye, right of it the right eye, straight ahead both. Each eye is capped at 250 Hz and rounded to 25 Hz steps.
  2. Stimulate. Restore the network to its clean state, then drive the looming-detector neurons of each eye (LPLC2 and LC4: 162 cells on the left, 152 on the right) with Poisson spikes at those rates.
  3. Simulate 100 ms of brain time (about a second of wall clock on the M1).
  4. Read out.
    • Turn signal = (DNa01 + DNb01) right − (DNa01 + DNb01) left. These steering neurons fire on the side opposite a one-sided threat. Above 0 Hz → right, below → left.
    • Jump signal = mean rate of the Giant Fiber (DNp01), the fly's real escape-jump neuron. Above 200 Hz → jump, which beats a turn.
    • Otherwise stay.
Ours, not the fly's

Four numbers: the gain (250 Hz) and falloff (cubic) of the eye input, and the two thresholds (0 Hz and 200 Hz). They were picked once from 768 candidates by a rule fixed in advance, on practice seeds 1000–1199, using a measured "response surface" as a fast stand-in for the brain, then checked against the real brain (stand-in predicted 118.7 average rows on seeds 1000–1019, the real brain did 118.0). Frozen: calibration/REPORT.md. They must never be retuned.

Known weaknesses (said on screen too)
  • It doesn't plan; it flees and may flee into another gap.
  • Input is crude: whole-eye stimulation, not a picture.
  • It leans right: on equal input to both eyes the turn signal is 0 to +20 Hz, never negative (its left eye has more looming cells). With a 0 Hz threshold, a hole straight ahead usually becomes a step right. The lean is the fly's; the threshold that exposes it is ours.
  • Its Giant Fiber fires at 100–200 Hz in the model, unrealistic for a real fly (about once per escape); we use it as a graded signal.
  • The steering role of DNa01/DNb01 comes from published studies, not from anything we verified.

Randomness only comes from the Poisson input, seeded per (track, row), so a fly run repeats exactly: the live run on track 1001 was move for move the same as the batch run two days earlier.

fly2: a second way in and out

Same brain, same simulator. What differs is what drives the eyes and which neurons we read.

  1. Input, "a sideways channel" (M3). A gap in the runner's own lane drives the looming cells of both eyes (LPLC2 and LC4), same as fly. A gap in a lane to the side (up to three lanes either way) also drives a second, more lateral pair of looming cells (LPLC4 and LC22) on that side's eye — input fly never gets. Each gap adds 250 / row² Hz to its channel, capped at 500 Hz and rounded to 100 Hz steps.
  2. Read-out. Turn signal = (DNa02 + DNa01 + DNg13) right − (DNa02 + DNa01 + DNg13) left — fly reads DNa01 and DNb01 instead. Jump signal = the Giant Fiber (DNp01), same as fly.
  3. Rule: dodge before jump. Turn if the turn signal is above 40 Hz either way; otherwise jump if the Giant Fiber is above 175 Hz; otherwise stay.
Ours, not the fly's

The sideways mapping, the read-out and the rule, plus four numbers: the gain (250 Hz) and falloff (row squared) of the input, and the two thresholds (40 Hz, 175 Hz). Picked once by a rule fixed before any measurement, from three candidate mappings measured on the real brain, on practice seeds 1000–1199, then played once on held-out seeds 1200–1399 (82.3 rows). Frozen: calibration/FLY2_REPORT.md. They must never be retuned.

Two controls come with the result. With no brain at all, our mapping and rule alone already reach 74 rows on the held-out seeds; the real wiring adds about 8 more. On shuffled wiring, the read-out neurons never fire at all, so that control shows only that the real wiring routes these eye cells to them, not how good the routing is. Most deaths are still the same kind as fly's: jumping into a gap (158 of 195 on held-out seeds).

Caveats
  • The winning turn threshold (40 Hz) sits at the top of the grid it was chosen from, so a higher one was never tried.
  • The held-out seeds were not wholly unseen: they had already been used by the research that chose the three candidate mappings.

10. The question sets, in detail

The plain pair, jev_plain and haiku_plain, are both told the same rules in the same words (bakeoff/players/briefing.py, written before any paid request) and receive the same JSON view:

{
  "lane": 4, "lanes": 12, "rows_survived": 37,
  "ahead": [
    {"row": 1, "gaps_relative": [-1, 0]},
    {"row": 2, "gaps_relative": []},
    {"row": 3, "gaps_relative": [2, 3]}, ...
  ],
  "actions": {"left": "move one lane left", "right": "move one lane right",
              "jump": "clear the next row, land on the one after", "stay": "run straight"}
}

The LLM (haiku_plain)

Claude Haiku 4.5, one request per row, system prompt = the rules, user message = the JSON, answer forced to {"action": "left" | "right" | "jump" | "stay"} by structured output. Anything else is logged as invalid.

jev_step1 (the one in the demo)

One request per row with four Nouls (a Noul is Jev's yes/no-as-probability question type):

gap_left : Would the action `left` land the runner on a gap, that is,
           does `ahead[0].gaps_relative` contain -1?
gap_stay : ... `stay` ... ahead[0] contains 0?
gap_right: ... `right` ... ahead[0] contains 1?
gap_jump : ... `jump` ... ahead[1] contains 0?

Code picks the action with the lowest probability after rounding to two decimals; ties go in the solver's order stay, left, right, jump. If any answer is missing, the move is invalid. The visor sprite's lit slit shows 1 minus the chosen action's probability, that is, how sure Jev is the move is safe.

Ours, not TypeSafe's

The question wording and the pick-the-lowest rule. It looks one step ahead only. The rounding is why it wanders: on safe rows answers like 0.03 and 0.04 rarely tie, so the "safest" move is often a sideways step.

Plain Jev (jev_plain, kept for comparison)

One Choice over the four actions plus two logging Nouls. Spike 02 showed why it failed: on the 55 dangerous states of track 1000 its Choice landed on a gap 29% of the time, about the same as always staying (33%). Jev's questions in one request run in parallel and can't see each other's answers, so the Nouls couldn't help the Choice. TypeSafe's own docs call the broad question the anti-pattern.

The four question sets (update 2a), played by three models

A question set is a wording plus a rule for turning the answers into a move. All four are ours, written once and given to every model unchanged (bakeoff/players/question_sets.py):

SetWhat it asks each rowThe rule
step14 questions: would each move land on a gap?take the least likely
guided1 question: which move?take the favourite
step28 questions: land on a gap, and be trapped after it?take the lowest combined risk
map42 questions: is each visible tile a gap?plan a path through what it read

The names are the skins of Brain Run's character select (decision 44; Brain Run was called Brain Battle until decision 51). Until 2026-09-25 the sets were composed, choice, two_step and reader, and the plain players were jev, haiku and glm: every run recorded so far is filed under those names (and Claude Haiku's under llm*, before decision 39). The tool reads them, and their cached answers, as the new names, and an old name still works in a command.

Each set is played by Jev (jev_*), by Claude Haiku (haiku_*) and by GLM Flash (glm_*), so the model is what differs — almost. Three things still differ besides the model, and the write-up must carry all three: the chat models are also given the briefing of the rules (Jev's yes/no questions carry only the question, except in the guided set); Jev's answers in one request are made in parallel and cannot see each other, while a chat model writes them all in one reply, each able to see the ones before it; and a chat model's probability is a number it states, while Jev's is Jev's own probability. Paid runs are done for practice seeds 1000–1004 (docs/COSTS.md).

One measurement to read carefully: the Brier score beside each player says how well calibrated its probabilities were. It compares two models within one set, never one set against another — about 90% of the map set's tiles are floor, so a player can score well there while dying on every track.

Money safety

Every paid request goes through bakeoff/clients/core.py: first a disk cache (key = sha256 of provider, model, senses and the full question set, so a changed prompt can never reuse an old answer), then a hard cap (--max-requests, per capped paid player, default 0 = replay only; Claude Haiku and GLM Flash are capped, Jev plays without a cap since decision 50 and its requests are still cached, counted and priced), then the live call, with SDK retries off so the cap is exact. A provider failure is logged as an error and the game runs stay (never the solver's move; being rescued would hide exactly what we want to see). Keys load from a git-ignored .env. The CLI refuses a live paid run on seeds below 1000 without --tournament.

11. Runs, records and replays

A run directory (runs/<timestamp>/) holds meta.json (status, git commit, arguments, model ids, fly constants) and one <player>.jsonl file with one line per move. Status goes running → completed | aborted | budget_exhausted | interrupted.

Each record mixes two moments: what the player saw when deciding (row, lane, senses, looming, questions) and what happened after (answers, chosen_action, executed_action, solver_action, alive, rows_survived, death_cause, latency_ms, usage, cache_hit, info). The full track is logged once per seed. Contract: docs/STEP_RECORD.md.

bakeoff/replay.py merges several run directories into one replay object (docs/REPLAY_DATA.md): tracks, episodes lined up by row, landing tiles, the scoreboard and whether its means are a fair comparison. bakeoff/view.py embeds that object, the JavaScript and the two fonts in one HTML file that never loads anything from the network.

The viewer's JavaScript is split into pure, tested modules: timeline.js (time is rows; a jump takes two ticks), tunnel.js (tile geometry, fixed camera), sprites.js (pixel figures), stage.js (overlapping runners, auto-focus), minds.js (the panels), log.js (one line per row of a mind's log), tabs.js and picker.js (the two tabs; who is in the tunnel), lobby.js (the cost estimate and the refusal text), bench_view.js (the benchmark's markup and charts) and feed.js, the only way frames reach app.js, either from the embedded replay or from the live stream. Each is tested with node --test, which uv run pytest runs; anything that came from a log is escaped before it becomes markup.

live serves the same page from 127.0.0.1 and pushes the same frame shapes over Server-Sent Events, standard library only. A page can carry a second data slot, the benchmark of the same runs, and a live run's last event carries the benchmark of what it just played — scored on the server, so the page never does arithmetic the Python does not.

12. Rules we hold ourselves to

  • Seed discipline. Seeds 1000+ are practice (1000–1399 were used to calibrate both flies). Tournament seeds are below 1000, and nothing is ever tuned on them. Prompts and rules are frozen before the tournament; bad results get named in the write-up, not fixed.
  • Honesty about the flies. Untrained, innate wiring only, for fly and fly2 alike. Anything that is our mapping rather than the fly's biology is labelled as ours, on screen and in the write-up.
  • Same words for every model. A question set is written once and given to Jev, Haiku and GLM unchanged; what still differs between them (the briefing, parallel answers, whose probability it is) is named wherever the numbers are shown.
  • Say how sure we are, where the number is read. No interval below five tracks, no verdict without one, and a count of how many verdicts chance alone would give among the pairs compared. A benchmark of one track says so above its own table.
  • Budget. No paid request without a cap and a cache (Jev, whose requests cost you nothing, needs only the cache, decision 50); no cap raised without your go-ahead.
  • One fly at a time on the 8 GB Mac (about 1 GB each).
  • How work gets done: brainstorm → spec → plan → one implementer and one reviewer per task → final review → PR. Never on main. Tests first; no test touches the network.
