The question

Which mind runs the tunnel furthest for what it costs and for how long it thinks: Jev, Claude Haiku, GLM Flash, an untrained fruit-fly connectome (two ways of feeding it), and three bots for scale.

Method

Every player ran the held-out tracks 100 to 199 of game v2: 150 rows each, getting harder until full difficulty at row 100. No player was tuned on them and none had played them before the study. A player sees six rows ahead and three lanes either side, and each row it stays, moves left or right, or jumps.

  • The free players ran all of them: the five Jev players ( tracks each), the two flies and the three bots.
  • Claude Haiku ran the first 15 (100 to 114), capped at 10 USD in the worst case, with four of its five question sets. The map set was left out: it is the priciest, and its Jev twin is among the weakest players (decision 54).
  • GLM Flash: to be settled from its free-tier trial on practice tracks (decision 53's rule: under 2% failed decisions on every question set).

Every comparison between two players uses only the tracks both ran, so anything about Claude Haiku rests on its tracks and free against free on . A mean is given with its 95% interval over tracks.

Cost is every decision at the listed price per request, answers replayed from the cache included, so a replay never looks cheaper. Claude Haiku's prices are measured; Jev's are an estimate, since its provider does not bill per request and costs this project nothing (decision 50). The flies, the bots and GLM Flash's free tier cost nothing. Speed is the median time a live decision took.

What is ours

  • Every question and every rule. The wording of each question set and the code that turns the answers into a move are this project's, not TypeSafe's or Anthropic's. Plain asks "which move?" once; guided asks one question naming the tile each move lands on; step 1 asks four yes/no questions (would this move land on a gap?) and code picks the least likely; step 2 asks eight, two moves ahead, and code takes the lowest combined risk; map asks about every visible tile and code plans a path.
  • What differs between a Jev player and its Claude Haiku twin besides the model: Claude Haiku is also given a briefing of the rules (Jev's yes/no questions carry only the question, except in the guided set); Jev answers each question separately, while Claude Haiku writes all its answers in one reply; and Claude Haiku's probability is a number it states.
  • The flies' input, read-out and thresholds. The wiring is the real fly's, untrained. How a gap becomes input to its eye (looming), which neurons are read as a turn or a jump, and the thresholds are ours. The fly's thresholds were fixed on game v1 practice tracks and never retuned; fly2's sideways channel and its numbers were fixed on practice tracks too, never on the held-out ones.
  • The solver searches what every player can see, not the whole track: a gap just out of sight can trap it, which is why it did not finish every track.
